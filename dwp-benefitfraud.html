Department for Work and Pensions

Making benefit fraud reporting work with incomplete information.

Redesigning a sensitive GOV.UK reporting service so people could submit useful information quickly, anonymously and without being blocked by details they did not know.

The challenge.

DWP wanted more people to report suspected benefit fraud online, reducing reliance on paper submissions and the call centre while improving the information received by investigation teams.

The existing service made that difficult.

People reporting fraud often had useful information, but they rarely knew every detail about the person they were reporting. They might know a nickname, an approximate address, where someone worked or a pattern of behaviour, without knowing a full legal name or date of birth.

The form treated those gaps as errors. Mandatory questions prevented people from continuing, even when the information they could provide might still support an investigation.

The challenge was to make reporting quicker and easier without reducing the quality of information collected.

Understanding why people struggled.

Working closely with user researchers, we built a picture of how the existing service performed using several sources of evidence.

We reviewed previous online submissions, including complete reports, partially completed forms and reports containing very little usable information.

We spent time with the call-centre team to understand the questions people asked, the information they struggled to provide and the operational pressure created by handling reports manually.

We also ran usability sessions with people who could imagine someone they might report, including participants who had previously used the service.

Four clear needs emerged:

  • People wanted to complete the report quickly, often using a mobile phone.
  • They needed to submit a report without knowing every personal detail.
  • They wanted confidence that the process was anonymous.
  • They needed reassurance that the information had been successfully handed over.

The research showed that the service had been structured around the information the organisation wanted, rather than the information people could realistically provide.

Designing around uncertainty.

Instead of forcing everyone through one long, fixed form, we redesigned the journey around what the person knew.

Common types of benefit fraud were introduced earlier, allowing the service to ask questions that were more relevant to the selected situation.

We reviewed which questions genuinely needed an answer and removed unnecessary blockers where people might not know exact details.

The redesigned journey allowed people to:

  • Provide a name, nickname or other identifying information.
  • Continue when exact details such as a date of birth were unknown.
  • Explain what they had observed in their own words.
  • See questions relevant to the type of report being made.
  • Understand that they could report anonymously.
  • Review their answers before submitting.

Clearer GOV.UK patterns and content also made the service feel more trustworthy and familiar, which was particularly important for a sensitive and anonymous reporting journey.

Improving the information received.

The goal was not simply to make the form shorter.

The service needed to balance two competing needs: reduce friction for the person reporting while collecting enough information for investigators to understand and act on the report.

We used evidence from previous submissions and call-centre conversations to identify the information that was most useful, then shaped questions around the way people naturally described what they knew.

This helped the service capture partial but potentially valuable information without presenting every missing detail as a failure.

I worked closely with the wider squad throughout the process, sharing user journeys, prototypes and research findings so that design decisions were understood across design, research, delivery and development.

Outcomes.

The redesigned service created a more focused and forgiving route for reporting suspected benefit fraud online.

The work delivered:

  • A shorter journey shaped around common reporting situations.
  • Fewer blockers caused by personal details the reporter did not know.
  • Questions tailored to the type of fraud being reported.
  • Clearer reassurance around anonymity and privacy.
  • A stronger sense of completion and successful handover.
  • Better alignment with established GOV.UK interaction patterns.
  • A service informed by historic submissions, call-centre insight and usability research.
  • More useful information structured for investigation teams.

The original project did not record a final measured increase in submissions, so the case study does not claim one. Its strongest evidence is the way the redesigned journey directly addressed the barriers uncovered through research.

Looking back.

This work reinforced the value of combining behavioural evidence, operational insight and direct user research.

Looking only at the existing form might have led us to improve its wording or visual design. Reviewing incomplete submissions and speaking with call-centre colleagues exposed a more fundamental problem: the service assumed that people would arrive with complete information.

Forms often fail because they are designed around the data an organisation wants rather than the knowledge a person can realistically provide.

The lesson I carried forward was simple:

Good service design makes uncertainty usable.
