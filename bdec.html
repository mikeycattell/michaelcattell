HMRC

Improving a complex investigation platform with users and delivery teams.

Leading design on a sensitive HMRC platform used by investigators, analysts and operational teams to review large volumes of complex information.

Role

Principal Product Designer

Organisation

HMRC

Stage

Discovery → Beta

Users

Investigators, analysts, agents and team leaders

Team

Multidisciplinary team of around 12 people, with me as the only designer

Focus

Evidence review, workflow design and AI-assisted investigation

The challenge.

When I joined the programme, the challenge was not simply to redesign an ageing interface. The platform had evolved over many years and, like many long-running internal systems, had accumulated usability challenges, inconsistencies and constraints that were affecting the day-to-day work of users.

The team had strong technical and operational knowledge, but there was a need for clearer design leadership to connect user needs, business priorities and delivery plans into a shared direction. Feedback loops with users had become inconsistent, technical constraints had limited what could be delivered and many users felt disconnected from the future of the product.

At the same time, the work itself was growing. Users were dealing with large, complex cases, reviewing significant volumes of information and trying to support investigations where accuracy, confidence and judgement really mattered. The product needed a new platform and a clearer design system, but more importantly, the team needed a structured way to understand how people were actually using it.

Understanding the work.

One of the advantages of joining an established team was the amount of knowledge already in the room. Developers, business analysts, product colleagues and users all understood different parts of the problem space. My role was not to arrive with all the answers, but to help bring those perspectives together through research, design decisions and clearer product direction.

Rather than starting with a list of requested features, I focused on understanding the day-to-day work. I spoke to investigators, analysts, agents and team leaders about what they actually did during the day, how investigations moved, where information came from, what slowed them down and where the existing product fitted into the wider process.

This helped the team see the product from a different angle. Users were not just struggling with individual screens; they were working around the product, creating spreadsheets, relying on memory, downloading documents and building their own processes to get the job done. That changed the conversation from isolated requests to a clearer picture of the work we needed to support.

What we found.

The research showed that many of the biggest problems were not new to users. They had often been living with them for years, partly because the legacy platform made change difficult and partly because there had not been a consistent design process for turning feedback into delivery.

Document review was slow, search and filtering were difficult to trust, the interface could feel overwhelming and important work often happened outside the platform because the system did not fully support it. Managers were also spending too much time allocating work and managing process, rather than focusing on quality, outputs and supporting their teams.

One of the clearest themes was confidence. Users were not asking for a shinier interface. They wanted a product that understood their work, used language they recognised and helped them move through large volumes of information without feeling lost.

Turning research into delivery.

A lot of the work became about joining things together. User pain points needed to be visible, but they also needed to connect to business needs, delivery priorities and the development roadmap. I mapped the main issues back to evidence from research, then worked with the team to turn those findings into practical design and delivery decisions.

That helped change the way features were discussed. Instead of asking whether something was interesting to build, we could ask whether it solved a real problem, whether users had asked for it directly or indirectly and whether it helped the programme move towards a more usable, trusted product.

I also took responsibility for prioritising design work and keeping it ahead of development. My aim was always to give the team enough clarity to build with confidence, rather than forcing design and development to make decisions at the same time under pressure.

What changed.

Since the early discovery work, the team has delivered a number of improvements shaped directly by user research. These include saved searches, quick filters, a column picker, better table density, document relationships, a resizable document viewer, improved annotation journeys and export functionality that users now rely on to support their wider investigation process.

Some of these improvements sound small on paper, but they matter because they sit inside repetitive, high-volume work. A bigger click area, clearer table state, better document viewing or faster annotation flow can make a meaningful difference when someone is reviewing large volumes of material every day.

The design system work has also been important. The previous product had language, column names and filters that often felt like they had been written around the system rather than the people using it. Part of the redesign has been about creating clearer rules, more human language and a more consistent interface that helps people understand what they are looking at and what they can do next.

Introducing AI carefully.

AI has naturally become part of the programme, but I have been careful to frame it in a way that supports users rather than replacing them. From previous work with AI-assisted workflows, I knew there could be real value in summaries, snippets and triage support, but only if the team understood where those tools should and should not be used.

The principle I have used throughout is simple: AI is for investigation, not evidence. It can help users find patterns, understand large volumes of information more quickly and decide where to look next, but it does not replace human judgement, professional experience or situational understanding.

This has been an important message for developers, stakeholders and users. The most valuable AI patterns are not always the most exciting ones. Often the best use of AI is quiet support that reduces effort and helps people make better decisions while keeping humans firmly in control.

Rebuilding relationships.

One of the most important changes was communication. I was not specifically asked to rebuild the relationship with users, but it became obvious very quickly that the product could not succeed unless people felt listened to again.

Working with the wider team, we established a more regular rhythm of user engagement and made sure feedback was presented back to stakeholders in a clear, structured way. That meant polished decks, clear recommendations and a much more deliberate approach to how we spoke about the work. On a programme like this, clarity matters because messy conversations can quickly damage confidence.

Over time, the tone changed. Early conversations could be understandably difficult because users were frustrated and sceptical. Now those sessions are much more collaborative and friendly. Users are helping shape future ideas, giving feedback continuously and actively encouraging other teams to use the new product.

Where we are now.

The new UI is currently in pilot and adoption is growing quickly. Teams are now requesting access because of the reputation the product has built, and the programme is managing demand carefully while testing how far the platform can scale across different teams and types of work.

Feedback has been extremely positive, particularly around the areas that came directly from user research, such as inline document viewing, improved filtering, summaries, annotation, export and the ability to manage work in one place rather than across several tools.

The platform is now supporting live operational work and is increasingly being used on larger and more complex investigations. Alongside improvements in user satisfaction and adoption, stakeholders are also beginning to see measurable operational benefits as the platform becomes more widely embedded within investigative teams.

The work has also helped improve the relationship between HMRC and the delivery team. Regular communication, visible progress and a stronger user-centred approach contributed to the team receiving supplier recognition within HMRC, with communication and value called out as part of that success.

The project is still ongoing, but the conversation has changed. At the start, users needed confidence that the product team understood them. Now they are asking when they can get more of it.

Looking back.

This project has reinforced how much of design leadership is about creating the conditions for good work to happen. A good interface matters, but the product only really started to move when the team had a clearer way to connect user feedback, operational knowledge, business priorities and delivery plans.

It has also reminded me that useful work is not always the most exciting work. There is always a temptation, especially around AI, to chase the most impressive feature. But the things that make the biggest difference are often much more practical: clearer language, better filters, faster document review, fewer workarounds and giving people confidence that the system is helping rather than getting in the way.

The most satisfying part of the project has been seeing the relationship change. Users who were once frustrated are now actively contributing ideas, helping shape the roadmap and telling other teams about the product. For me, that is the strongest sign that the work is moving in the right direction.
