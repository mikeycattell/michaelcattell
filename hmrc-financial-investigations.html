HM Revenue & Customs

Simplifying complex financial investigations.

Reconnecting delivery with investigators to create a faster, safer and more evidence-led way of carrying out complex financial investigations.

Sole designer in a multidisciplinary product team redesigning how complex financial investigations are carried out across HMRC.

The challenge.

When I joined HMRC, I was the only designer on a multidisciplinary team responsible for redesigning how investigators carried out complex financial investigations.

The product supported investigators, analysts and managers working across HMRC, with an initial pilot of around 30 users and an ambition to scale nationally to more than 300. The long-term vision extended beyond investigators, eventually supporting the full journey through to lawyers and court proceedings.

I was originally hired to improve the interface.

It didn't take long to realise the interface wasn't the biggest problem.

The team genuinely cared about building a good product, but there was very little evidence behind many of the decisions. Conversations with users happened infrequently, feedback rarely influenced priorities and features were often built because they sounded useful rather than because anyone had proven they solved a real problem.

The experience reflected that.

Investigators worked across three different systems, downloaded evidence onto their machines, opened multiple browser tabs and stitched information together themselves. It wasn't just slow; it was fragmented, inconsistent and difficult to trust.

The programme didn't simply need better screens.

It needed someone to reconnect delivery with the people actually doing the work.

My role.

Although I joined as the team's UI designer, I quickly became responsible for far more than interface design.

As the only designer on the programme, I led user research, interaction design, interface design, service design, prototyping, usability testing and the evolution of the product's design system.

I also became heavily involved in product planning and prioritisation.

Rather than design being something that happened after decisions had already been made, it became part of deciding what should be built in the first place. Research findings, user observations and prototype feedback became regular inputs into planning sessions, helping the team prioritise problems instead of assumptions.

One of the biggest changes wasn't visual.

It was cultural.

We introduced regular fortnightly research sessions where previously there had been none. Instead of presenting finished work to users, we involved them throughout delivery, testing ideas early, iterating quickly and sharing what we'd learnt back with both the team and stakeholders.

Over time, users stopped feeling like people we occasionally demonstrated work to.

They became partners in shaping the product.

The impact.

The interface changed dramatically, but the biggest improvement wasn't visual.

Investigators who previously worked across three disconnected systems could now complete the majority of their work within a single workflow. Downloads, multiple browser tabs and unnecessary context switching were removed, allowing people to stay focused on the investigation itself rather than the software supporting it.

Research also began changing delivery.

Instead of long feedback cycles and assumptions, the team had a regular rhythm of testing, learning and improving.

The results were significant.

  • Pilot users estimated key investigation activities could now be completed in around 4–5 days, compared with roughly two weeks previously.
  • Three separate systems were consolidated into a single workflow.
  • Fortnightly research sessions replaced an ad-hoc approach to gathering feedback.
  • The pilot became so successful that several users joined the team in presenting the product to future users, sharing their own experiences rather than relying solely on the delivery team.

The programme itself has ambitions to recover hundreds of millions of pounds in tax revenue.

While I can't attribute those outcomes to design alone, helping investigators work faster, with greater confidence and within a more secure environment became a meaningful part of that wider goal.

Looking back.

I joined believing I'd spend most of my time redesigning an interface.

Instead, I spent most of my time building trust.

Trust between users and the team.

Trust in research.

Trust that slowing down long enough to understand people's work would ultimately help us deliver faster.

The screens matter.

The workflows matter.

But if there's one thing this programme reinforced, it's that good design isn't just about making software easier to use.

It's about helping teams make better decisions together.
